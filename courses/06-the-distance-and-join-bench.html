The distance-and-join bench

Everything the five sessions claimed is measured tonight, on the real geometry, in this page. 332 URA subzone centroids against 613 MRT station exits: the nearest exit and the exits within 500 m, computed five ways, from a flat constant on raw degrees up to a 500 m grid index over projected metres. Every exit joined to its subzone three ways. Then two buttons that should make the bench report a worse number: one moves the same points to 60 deg N and watches the raw-degree error grow by a factor of two hundred; the other swaps latitude and longitude and watches every subzone lose its exits. A Python reference run agrees with this engine on all 142 numbers it prints. Nothing here is modelled: the data is real, the arithmetic is live, and the licence line is on the widget.

Where we start

Arif's first question in session 1 was "how far is each estate from its nearest MRT exit?". Rivermont is constructed; the data it asks about is real: the URA Master Plan 2019 subzone boundaries (332 polygons, simplified to 5,326 vertices for the page), the LTA MRT station exits (613 points) and, for session 5's join, the NEA dengue clusters of 2026-10-22 and the Census 2020 population, all from data.gov.sg under the Singapore Open Data Licence v1.0. The bench answers Arif's question five times, each with a different idea of what "distance" means or how to find it, and scores every answer against SVY21 metres.

Part 1 · covers what the bench computes

How a map becomes numbers

6 min live

The query set is fixed so every rung is comparable: for each of the 332 subzones, the centroid of its rings; for each centroid, the nearest of the 613 exits and the count of exits within 500 m. Those are 203,516 pairs if you check all of them. Five definitions, the same in the browser and in the Python reference that checked every number on this page.

LiveThree numbers to read any rung3 min▶

Error against the reference. Each rung's nearest-exit distance minus the projected-metre distance for the same centroid, averaged over 332 and at its worst. The sign matters: both shortcuts are long, not random, which tells you the mechanism is systematic. On the reference rung this is 0 by construction.

Nearest exit changed, and catchment counts changed. A distance can be wrong by 50 m and still name the right exit. These two counts say when the wrong formula changes the decision, not just the number: raw degrees and haversine each pick a different nearest exit for 5 of the 332 centroids, and change the 500 m count for 2.

Distance evaluations. How many pairs actually ran the formula. The first three rungs run all 203,516. The bounding box runs 888 for the catchment query; the grid runs 5,528 for nearest and catchment together. Same answers.

Self-studyWhat the shipped geometry is, and is not3 min▶

The 332 subzone boundaries were simplified from 58,530 vertices to 5,326 with a 0.0003 deg tolerance, about 33 m, and quantised to 0.00001 deg, about 1.1 m, so the page loads 135 KB instead of 3 MB. Twenty-two tiny rings collapsed to nothing and were dropped, all slivers or islets. The cost is visible in the join: 3 of the 613 exits fall outside every simplified boundary and 2 fall inside two, where neighbouring simplified edges overlap by a few metres. The widget says the tolerance; a result built on simplified geometry should always say so.

Centroids are planar, in degrees, which is fine at 1 deg N and said on the widget. A concave or multi-part subzone can have its centroid outside itself (session 2), and the nearest exit to that centroid is still a well-defined number; it is just not "the subzone's nearest exit" in the sense a resident would mean. That is a boundary choice, and the bench makes it out loud rather than hiding it.

Build-along · the bench

Five rungs, 203,516 pairs, computed as you click

★ 26 min · the main event

The bench opens on rung 1 with Bishan East selected. Read the rule line first, then the verdict, then the six numbers, then the subzone card and the map. Climb the rungs in order and say out loud what changed and what did not before moving on. Pick other subzones from the list: the five whose nearest exit changes between rungs 1 and 3 are worth finding. Then press the two buttons at the bottom.

Read it in this order. On rung 1, Bishan East's nearest exit is Bishan MRT Station Exit D at 345.9 m by raw degrees, 345.4 m in projected metres, one exit within 500 m under both. Half a metre on this subzone, 57 m on the worst one: the error of a shortcut is a distribution, and the worst case is what decides whether a decision flips.

Part 2 · covers what the five rungs show

Wrong by metres, or right with 2.7% of the work

6 min live

The bench computes these live; the numbers here are the ones it prints, written down so they can be argued with after class. They agree with the independent Python run on every value.

LiveFinding 1: the sphere is the bigger error at 1 deg N2 min▶

Raw degrees with one constant are 3.79 m too long on average and 57.03 m at worst; the median nearest exit is 611.23 m against 610.38 m projected. Haversine is 2.57 m too long on average (2.86 m in absolute terms) and 42.95 m at worst, median 610.43 m. The two shortcuts differ by about a metre on average. The cosine that haversine handles and raw degrees ignore is worth 0.03% at this latitude; the ellipsoid that neither handles is worth 0.7% on north-south distances, and a sphere of one radius cannot get both axes right. Session 3's claim was that near the equator the ellipsoid matters more than the latitude. Measured.

Both shortcuts change the nearest exit for 5 of 332 centroids and the 500 m count for 2. Small, and not zero: a desk reporting "estates more than 500 m from an exit" to Priya gets two of them wrong with either shortcut.

LiveFinding 2: an index changes the work, not the answer2 min▶

Rung 4 keeps projected metres and asks a cheaper question first: is the exit inside a 500 m square around the centroid? Only 888 pairs are, so only 888 run the distance formula for the catchment query, and the counts are identical: 701 exits within 500 m, 0 subzones differing. Rung 5 buckets the 613 exits into 500 m cells once, then for each centroid reads the 3 by 3 cells around it for the catchment and scans outward ring by ring for the nearest, stopping when the best distance found is shorter than the next ring could offer. 5,528 evaluations for both queries, the same 332 nearest exits, the same 701. The pattern is candidate-then-exact, and it is what every spatial index does underneath.

Self-studyFinding 3: point in polygon three ways2 min▶

Joining the 613 exits to their subzones by brute force runs 203,516 ray-casting tests and finds 608 exits inside exactly one subzone, 3 inside none and 2 inside two (the simplification artefacts above). Testing each exit against the 332 bounding boxes first gives 1,283 box hits, of which 671 are false, a box containing a point the polygon does not; the exact test then runs 1,283 times instead of 203,516. The 500 m grid over polygon boxes gives 2,396 exact tests after one cell lookup per exit, more than the box method because cells are coarser than boxes, but with no 332-box scan at all. All three agree on every exit. A spatial join that disagrees with brute force on any row is wrong, not fast.

Real world

Kian Teck, in Jurong West. Select it on the bench. An industrial subzone with 40 residents in the Census 2020 table, which session 5 turns into the darkest cell on a rate map from just 2 dengue cases. Here it is a reminder that a centroid-to-exit distance is a fact about geometry, and that the question "how far is the estate from an exit" assumes there is an estate. The bench answers the geometry; the desk still has to ask whether the question fits the zone.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever and the break button

Making the bench report a worse number

6 min live

A bench that can only confirm what you expect measures nothing. Two buttons exist to make it fail. The first keeps every point exactly where it is relative to the others and translates all of them 58.6333 deg north, so the SVY21 origin sits at 60 deg N: a constructed position, nothing in Singapore moved, and the widget says so. The second swaps the two coordinate columns on the exits.

LiveThe anti-lever: raw degrees at 60 deg N2 min▶

What it does: adds 58.6333 deg to every latitude, centroids and exits alike, and recomputes nearest exits by raw degrees and by haversine. Haversine is the reference here because SVY21 is defined for Singapore and nowhere else; the shift itself is constructed. What it prints: at 1 deg 22' N, raw degrees against haversine have a mean absolute error of 1.22 m, worst 14.08 m, and 0 nearest exits change. At 60 deg N the mean error is 276.03 m, the worst 2,384.4 m, and 124 of 332 nearest exits change. The formula is identical in both runs. The cosine went from 0.99972 to 0.5.

The honest nuance: this does not say raw degrees are useless, it says their error is a function of latitude, and that a method validated on Singapore data has been validated only at 1 deg N. A desk that inherits code from a northern-hemisphere project, or lends its own code north, should expect this number, not the Singapore one.

LiveThe break button: swap latitude and longitude2 min▶

What it does: every exit's longitude becomes its latitude and the other way round, then the join and the nearest query run again. What it prints: the largest "latitude" is now 103.989 deg, which does not exist, so the swap is detectable before any distance is computed, by the range check from session 1. Joined anyway, 332 of 332 subzones contain zero exits and the median nearest exit by haversine is 9,531 km away.

The bench is supposed to report that. A bench that still showed 701 exits within 500 m after the swap would be measuring something other than the geometry. Press it once a session: knowing what the failure looks like is how you recognise a quieter version of it in a real file, where only two rows are swapped and the medians barely move.

Self-studyHow the bench was checked2 min▶

The same engine file runs in this browser and under a server-side runtime, and a separate pure-Python implementation runs the same ladder in the same order. The two outputs are compared by value, 142 numbers, integers exactly and floating-point values within a millionth of a metre, and the comparison script first plants a 0.1% fault in a copy and confirms it is caught. On load, the engine tests itself: the SVY21 origin must land on (28,001.642, 38,744.572), the haversine antipode must equal pi times the radius, and ray casting must be right on a notched square and a square with a hole. Shift the false easting by one metre and the self-test fails. The widget prints the result of that test at the bottom, every time the page opens.

The sentence to use with a sceptical GM. "The distances are in SVY21 metres, checked against the projection's own origin; the shortcut version would have been out by up to 57 m here, and the index gives the same answers with 3% of the work. If we ever run this for a city at 60 deg N, the shortcut would be out by kilometres."

After the bench

  2. Find the two. Which two subzones change their 500 m count between raw degrees and projected metres? How far from 500 m is the exit that moved?

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Raw degrees are 57 m out at worst in Singapore and haversine 43 m. A colleague says haversine "fixes the latitude problem, so it should be nearly exact here". What is accurate?

Measured: mean error +3.79 m against +2.57 m, worst 57.03 m against 42.95 m. Haversine is better, by about a metre on average, because the thing it fixes is small here. At 60 deg N the same comparison is 276 m against haversine's reference, and the order of importance flips.

2 · The grid index runs 5,528 distance evaluations instead of 203,516 and returns the same 332 nearest exits and the same 701 catchment exits. What does that prove?

Accuracy comes from the formula and the projection (rung 3); the index only decides which pairs get the formula. The bench prints both numbers so the two cannot be confused.

3 · After the break button, 332 of 332 subzones contain zero exits and the median nearest exit is 9,531 km away. What is the right conclusion?

A bench that could not report a worse number would be measuring nothing. The swap is the commonest coordinate error, its signature is an impossible latitude, and the gate from session 1 names it at row 1.
