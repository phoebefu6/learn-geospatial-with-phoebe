Learn Geospatial Data with Phoebe · Session 3 of 6

Distance three ways

Arif's question from session 1 is finally answerable: how far is each estate from its nearest MRT exit? The honest answer is "it depends which distance", and tonight there are three. Multiply degree differences by one constant and take Pythagoras. Treat the Earth as a sphere and take the great circle. Move both points into SVY21 metres and take Pythagoras there. On 332 subzone centroids against 613 exits the three answers differ by metres, not kilometres, and the order of the errors is not the one most people expect. Then we move the same points to 60 deg N and watch the cheapest method fall apart, for a reason that fits on one line: the width of a degree of longitude is 111.32 km times the cosine of the latitude.

Part 1 · covers one degree is not one length

The cosine that sets the price of a shortcut

5 min live

Session 1 planted the fact; tonight it does the work. At the equator one degree of longitude is 111.32 km, the circumference of the WGS 84 ellipsoid divided by 360. Meridians are not parallel lines, they meet at the pole, so the distance between two of them shrinks as you go north, and the shrink is exactly cos(latitude). One degree of latitude is a different length again, about 110.57 km near the equator, because the Earth is flattened and a meridian arc there is shorter than a parallel. Any formula that multiplies degrees by one constant has quietly assumed cos(latitude) is 1 and the two degrees are equal. Both assumptions are almost true at Rivermont's constructed desk in Singapore, and that "almost" is the subject of the session.

LiveTwo reasons the shortcut almost works at 1 deg N3 min▶

Take the flat shortcut apart. It says east-west metres = degrees of longitude x 111,320 and north-south metres = degrees of latitude x 111,320. The first line is off by the cosine: at 1 deg 22' N the cosine is 0.99972, so a degree of longitude is 111.29 km and the constant is 30 m long per degree, 0.03%. The second line is off by the flattening: a degree of latitude near the equator is 110.57 km, so the constant is 750 m long per degree, 0.7%. Across a nearest-exit distance of a few hundred metres both errors are centimetres to metres. That is why the shortcut survives in so many spreadsheets, and why Mei does not throw it out: she measures it.

Measured on the real data, nearest exit per subzone centroid by raw degrees is 3.79 m too long on average against SVY21 metres, 57.03 m too long at worst, and 5 of the 332 subzones get a different nearest exit. Not nothing, not a disaster. The point of tonight is to know which of those two numbers applies to the question Arif asked.

Self-studyWhy the error is positive, and why it grows with the bearing3 min▶

Both of the shortcut's constants are too large for Singapore, so every raw-degree distance is a little longer than the projected one: the mean error is +3.79 m, a bias, not noise. How much longer depends on direction. A pair of points due east-west is stretched by 0.03%; a pair due north-south by 0.7%. The worst case of 57.03 m is a centroid whose nearest exit sits a long way off and mostly north or south of it. If you ever see a flat-degree distance that is shorter than the projected one in Singapore, something else is wrong: a swapped axis, a different file, a typo in the constant.

This is also why "mean error" and "worst error" are both on the table. The mean tells you the bias of the method; the worst tells you whether a decision can flip. Arif's question is a ranking (which exit is nearest), and rankings flip on the worst case, not the mean.

Real world

The constant that moved cities. A familiar pattern at any desk that inherits code: a distance function with 111,320 hard-coded, written for one city, reused for a dataset about another at a very different latitude. Nothing errors. Every east-west distance comes out too long by exactly 1 / cos(latitude), and the "nearest" anything is quietly wrong for a share of the rows. The fix is not a better constant. It is a formula that carries the latitude, or a projection chosen for the place.

Part 2 · covers the three formulas and what each assumes

Flat, round, or flattened and projected

6 min live

Each distance formula is a statement about the shape of the Earth. Raw degrees say it is a flat grid with one scale. Haversine says it is a sphere of one radius, 6,371,008.8 m, with the cosine handled inside the formula. SVY21 says it is the WGS 84 ellipsoid, flattened by 1/298.26, unrolled onto a plane by the transverse Mercator projection whose parameters session 1 typed in. At Rivermont's constructed desk Mei runs all three on the real centroids and exits and lines them up against the third, which is the reference because it is the formula Singapore's own survey uses, with the control point from session 1 behind it.

LiveHaversine: the cosine handled, the sphere kept3 min▶

The haversine formula is the standard "proper" distance between two latitude-longitude pairs: a = sin^2(dphi/2) + cos(phi1) cos(phi2) sin^2(dlambda/2), then d = 2 R atan2(sqrt a, sqrt(1 - a)), with R = 6,371,008.8 m, the mean radius of the Earth (reported). The cos(phi1) cos(phi2) term is the cosine from Part 1, built in, so haversine is right about the width of a degree of longitude at every latitude. It is usually credited to Sinnott (1984, reported). It is also wrong about something, by construction: one radius cannot make a degree of latitude 110.57 km and a degree of longitude at the equator 111.32 km at the same time. A sphere has no flattening.

On the real data that shows up as a mean error of +2.57 m against SVY21 metres (mean absolute 2.86 m), worst 42.95 m, and the same 5 of 332 nearest exits changing as raw degrees. Against raw degrees directly, haversine differs by 1.22 m on average and 14.08 m at worst, and not one nearest exit changes between them. In Singapore, haversine's whole improvement over the shortcut is the cosine, and the cosine here is 0.99972.

Self-studyProjected metres as the reference, and the ellipsoidal answer that is not implemented4 min▶

Rung 3 is two steps: run both points through svy21() from session 1, then Pythagoras on the eastings and northings. It is the reference not because projection is perfect, every map projection distorts something, but because SVY21 was designed for exactly this patch of ground: a transverse Mercator with its central meridian at 103 deg 50' E and scale factor 1, so the distortion across Singapore's roughly 50 km is far below the metre-scale differences being measured, and the origin control point (28,001.642, 38,744.572) proves the arithmetic. The median nearest exit under it is 610.38 m and the mean 975.88 m, for 203,516 distance evaluations (332 x 613).

There is a fourth formula the session describes and does not implement. Vincenty (1975, reported) solves the inverse problem on the ellipsoid itself: the shortest path between two points on WGS 84, iteratively, with sub-millimetre accuracy claimed (reported). It is the right tool when there is no local projection to reach for, say two points on different continents, and the wrong amount of machinery for 613 exits inside one projection's area of use. Say "the ellipsoidal great-circle answer exists and is iterative", then use the projection you have. One seam: taking a subzone's centroid as "the subzone's point" is itself a boundary choice, and the depth of that argument (the modifiable areal unit problem, Openshaw 1984, reported) lives next door in Smart City's zones and joins session.

Name the shape when you name the formula. "I used haversine" is a statement that the Earth is a sphere. "I used raw degrees" is a statement that it is flat. Neither is wrong as an engineering choice; both are wrong as an unstated assumption. Mei's notes carry three lines, one per function, each ending with the shape it assumes and the error it was measured to have.

Part 3 · covers the same points at 60 deg N

Move the island north and watch the shortcut break

4 min live

The shortcut's error is small in Singapore because cos(1 deg 22') is 0.99972. To see what it costs elsewhere, Mei at Rivermont's constructed desk does something no real dataset would: she adds 58.6333 deg to every latitude, centroids and exits alike, so the SVY21 origin sits on 60 deg N, where the cosine is 0.5. The points keep their shape in degrees. The formulas do not change. Only the latitude does. Projected metres cannot follow, SVY21 is defined for Singapore only, so the comparison at 60 deg N is raw degrees against haversine, the sphere that at least knows about the cosine.

Contains information from Master Plan 2019 Subzone Boundary (No Sea) (GEOJSON) and LTA MRT Station Exit (GEOJSON) accessed on 2026-10-22 from data.gov.sg which is made available under the terms of the Singapore Open Data Licence version 1.0 https://data.gov.sg/open-data-licence

LiveWhat 124 of 332 means for a ranking2 min▶

At 1 deg 22' N, raw degrees and haversine disagree by 1.22 m on average and never change a nearest exit. At 60 deg N the same two functions on the same points disagree by 276.03 m on average, 2,384.4 m at worst, and 124 of the 332 subzones get a different nearest exit. The mean error grew by a factor of 226; more than a third of the answers changed. Nothing in the code is latitude-aware except haversine's cosine term, and that is the entire difference. A method that is "fine" is fine at a latitude. Write the latitude range next to the method in the notes, and treat a dataset from a different range as a different problem.

Self-studyWhy the order of errors flips2 min▶

Part 2 said that near the equator the sphere is the bigger error and the cosine the smaller one. The 60 deg N experiment is the other half of that sentence. The sphere's error comes from flattening and is a fraction of a percent everywhere on Earth. The cosine's error is 1 - cos(latitude) on the east-west axis: 0.03% at 1 deg 22' N, 50% at 60 deg N. One of the two errors is roughly constant and the other runs from nothing to half, so there must be a latitude where they cross, and it is not far north of Singapore. South of it, choose the ellipsoid over the sphere and you gain more than choosing the sphere over the flat grid. North of it, the cosine dominates and any formula that carries it, haversine included, is a large improvement on the shortcut. The projection, where one exists for the place, is right about both.

Real world

Arif's follow-up (constructed). Arif asks why the desk bothers with the third formula if the first two agree with it to a few metres. Load both files and give every subzone one point. The subzones are d_8594ae9ff96d0c708bc2af633048edfb and the exits d_b39d3a0871985372d7e1637193335da5, both in the download folder from sessions 1 and 2, both [longitude, latitude] on WGS 84. The centroid is the area-weighted planar centroid of every outer ring minus every hole, computed in degrees, which is fine at 1 deg N and said so. Run with -I as before.

★ Step 1 · centroidsimport json, math, sys sz = json.load(open(sys.argv[1], encoding="utf-8"))["features"] # URA subzones, 332 ex = json.load(open(sys.argv[2], encoding="utf-8"))["features"] # LTA MRT exits, 613 def ring(r): # shoelace: signed area and the centroid sums of one ring a = cx = cy = 0.0 for i in range(len(r) - 1): x0, y0 = r[i][:2]; x1, y1 = r[i + 1][:2] w = x0 * y1 - x1 * y0 a += w; cx += (x0 + x1) * w; cy += (y0 + y1) * w return a / 2, cx / 6, cy / 6 def centroid(geom): # outer rings add, holes subtract, whatever their winding polys = [geom["coordinates"]] if geom["type"] == "Polygon" else geom["coordinates"] A = CX = CY = 0.0 for rings in polys: for k, r in enumerate(rings): a, cx, cy = ring(r) s = (1 if k == 0 else -1) * (1 if a > 0 else -1) A += s * a; CX += s * cx; CY += s * cy return CX / A, CY / A cents = [(f["properties"]["SUBZONE_N"], *centroid(f["geometry"])) for f in sz] pts = [(p["properties"]["STATION_NA"] + " " + p["properties"]["EXIT_CODE"], *p["geometry"]["coordinates"][:2]) for p in ex] print(len(cents), len(pts)) # 332 613 print(cents[0]) # the first subzone's name and two degrees; your numbers will differ

Write the three functions, each with its shape in the comment. The first is the constant from Part 1 on both axes. The second is haversine with R = 6,371,008.8 m. The third is svy21() from session 1 (the EPSG:3414 series, repeated here so this file runs on its own) followed by Pythagoras. Then test them on one degree of latitude from the origin: the projection should give about 110,574 m, the meridian arc; the constant gives 111,320 by construction; the sphere gives a third number between them, because one radius cannot match both.

★ Step 2 · three distancesDEG_M = 111320.0 # rung 1: one constant, both axes (assumes a flat grid) R = 6371008.8 # rung 2: sphere radius, metres (IUGG mean radius, reported) def rawdeg(lon1, lat1, lon2, lat2): # assumes: flat, cos(latitude) = 1, one degree = one length return math.hypot((lon2 - lon1) * DEG_M, (lat2 - lat1) * DEG_M) def haversine(lon1, lat1, lon2, lat2): # assumes: a sphere; the cosine is inside the formula p1, p2 = math.radians(lat1), math.radians(lat2) dp, dl = p2 - p1, math.radians(lon2 - lon1) a = math.sin(dp / 2) ** 2 + math.cos(p1) * math.cos(p2) * math.sin(dl / 2) ** 2 return 2 * R * math.atan2(math.sqrt(a), math.sqrt(1 - a)) A_, F_ = 6378137.0, 1 / 298.257223563 # WGS 84 ellipsoid; SVY21 / EPSG:3414 as in session 1 LAT0, LON0 = 1 + 22 / 60, 103 + 50 / 60 FE, FN, K0 = 28001.642, 38744.572, 1.0 E2 = 2 * F_ - F_ * F_; EP2 = E2 / (1 - E2); E4 = E2 * E2; E6 = E4 * E2 def merid(phi): return A_ * ((1 - E2/4 - 3*E4/64 - 5*E6/256) * phi - (3*E2/8 + 3*E4/32 + 45*E6/1024) * math.sin(2*phi) + (15*E4/256 + 45*E6/1024) * math.sin(4*phi) - (35*E6/3072) * math.sin(6*phi)) M0 = merid(math.radians(LAT0)) def svy21(lon, lat): phi, lam = math.radians(lat), math.radians(lon) s, c, t = math.sin(phi), math.cos(phi), math.tan(phi) n = A_ / math.sqrt(1 - E2 * s * s); tt = t * t; cc = EP2 * c * c a1 = (lam - math.radians(LON0)) * c; a2 = a1*a1; a3 = a2*a1; a4 = a3*a1; a5 = a4*a1; a6 = a5*a1 x = K0 * n * (a1 + (1 - tt + cc) * a3/6 + (5 - 18*tt + tt*tt + 72*cc - 58*EP2) * a5/120) y = K0 * (merid(phi) - M0 + n * t * (a2/2 + (5 - tt + 9*cc + 4*cc*cc) * a4/24 + (61 - 58*tt + tt*tt + 600*cc - 330*EP2) * a6/720)) return FE + x, FN + y assert svy21(LON0, LAT0) == (28001.642, 38744.572) # the control point, by definition def projected(lon1, lat1, lon2, lat2): # assumes: WGS 84 ellipsoid, projected to SVY21 metres e1, n1 = svy21(lon1, lat1); e2, n2 = svy21(lon2, lat2) return math.hypot(e2 - e1, n2 - n1) for f in (rawdeg, haversine, projected): # one degree of latitude, due north from the origin print(f"{f.__name__:<10} {f(LON0, 0.0, LON0, 1.0):10.1f} m") # rawdeg 111320.0 m the constant, by construction # haversine (your output) one radius: between the other two, right about neither # projected ~110574 m the meridian arc near the equator, 110.57 km

Run the loop three times. Every centroid against every exit is 332 x 613 = 203,516 distance evaluations per formula. Session 4 is about not doing this; tonight it is fine, it is a second or two, and it is the honest baseline every shortcut there will be checked against.

★ Step 3 · nearest exit, three waysdef nearest(cents, pts, dist): out, evals = [], 0 for name, clon, clat in cents: best = None for ename, elon, elat in pts: d = dist(clon, clat, elon, elat); evals += 1 if best is None or d < best[1]: best = (ename, d) out.append(best) return out, evals rows = {} for k, f in (("raw", rawdeg), ("haversine", haversine), ("projected", projected)): rows[k], evals = nearest(cents, pts, f) print(k, evals, "distance evaluations") # 203516 each

Compare against the reference, three numbers per formula. Mean error (the bias), worst absolute error (what can flip a ranking), and how many subzones get a different nearest exit. The canon lines are the bench's on the shipped geometry; expect yours to land near them, not on them.

★ Step 4 · the comparisondef median(xs): s = sorted(xs); n = len(s) return s[n // 2] if n % 2 else (s[n // 2 - 1] + s[n // 2]) / 2 ref = rows["projected"] for k in ("raw", "haversine", "projected"): r = rows[k] errs = [a[1] - b[1] for a, b in zip(r, ref)] changed = sum(1 for a, b in zip(r, ref) if a[0] != b[0]) print(f"{k:<10} median {median([a[1] for a in r]):7.2f} m mean err {sum(errs) / len(errs):+6.2f} m" f" worst {max(abs(e) for e in errs):6.2f} m nearest changed {changed:3d} of {len(r)}") # Bench canon on the shipped geometry (simplified 0.0003 deg, quantised); your raw-file numbers will differ a little: # raw median 611.23 m mean err +3.79 m worst 57.03 m nearest changed 5 of 332 # haversine median 610.43 m mean err +2.57 m worst 42.95 m nearest changed 5 of 332 # projected median 610.38 m mean err +0.00 m worst 0.00 m nearest changed 0 of 332

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Haversine fixes the cosine that the raw-degree shortcut ignores. On the real Singapore data it improves the mean error from +3.79 m to +2.57 m, and the two agree to 1.22 m on average. Why so little?

Haversine's entire improvement over the shortcut is the cosine, and near the equator the cosine is almost 1. What is left, +2.57 m, is the sphere against the ellipsoid, a 110.57 against 111.32 km mismatch that one radius cannot fix. A points at a constant that is not the issue; C confuses precision with the formula; D is invented.

2 · The same 332 centroids and 613 exits are shifted north by 58.6333 deg so the origin sits at 60 deg N. Nothing in the code changes. What happens to raw degrees against haversine?

The points keep their shape in degrees but not on the ground: at 60 deg N a degree of longitude is half as wide, and the shortcut's constant does not know that. Haversine does, through its cosine term, so the gap between them is the cosine's whole effect. A is the trap the figure is drawn to break; B and C have the mechanism backwards.

3 · Priya asks which of the three distance columns is "right". What is the defensible answer for Arif's nearest-exit question in Singapore?

The reference is the projection designed for the place and proved by its control point; the other two are measured against it, not assumed. B promotes a sphere over the ellipsoid; C skips the worst case, which is what flips a ranking; D changes the question (as-the-crow-flies distance was what Arif asked, and routing is a different subject).
